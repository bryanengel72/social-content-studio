# Brand Voice

This is the difference between content that sounds like the photographer and
content that sounds like a robot wearing the photographer's logo. Get it wrong
and every post reads as generic AI filler, the audience feels it, and the whole
calendar quietly stops working. Get it right and the posts sound like a real
person the client already half-knows by the time they book.

## Calibrate from real samples, not from a vibe

Do not guess the voice. At setup, get **three to five captions the photographer
has actually written** — their best real ones, or ones they wish they'd written.
Read them for the concrete, transferable rules:

- **Sentence length and rhythm.** Short and punchy? Long and warm? Fragments?
- **Person and pronoun.** "I" / "we" / neither. Do they address the reader as
  "you", or talk about the work in the third person?
- **Formality.** Lowercase-casual, or composed and polished?
- **Emoji.** How many, which ones, where — or none at all.
- **Recurring phrases and tics.** A sign-off, a way they describe light, a word
  they overuse. These are fingerprints; keep them.
- **What they never do.** No exclamation points? No hashtags in the caption body?

Write these down in the brand config and in LEARNINGS.md, and generate against
them every time. When in doubt, show the photographer two options and ask which
sounds more like them — their gut is the ground truth, and it sharpens the model
of their voice for next time.

## The anti-AI-slop rules

The default failure mode of generated captions is a specific, recognisable
texture. Avoid it on purpose:

- **No emoji vomit.** "✨📸 Magic happens when… 💫🙌" is the tell. Match the
  photographer's real emoji rate, which is usually far lower than the model's
  instinct.
- **No hollow superlatives.** "absolutely stunning", "pure magic", "obsessed
  with this one", "the most incredible". These say nothing and everyone writes
  them. Say the specific thing instead.
- **No manufactured emotion.** Don't narrate feelings the photographer didn't
  express. A headshot session is not "a journey".
- **No engagement-bait clichés.** "Drop a ❤️ if…", "Tag someone who…", "Comment
  YES below" — unless the photographer genuinely talks that way, it reads as
  desperate.
- **No fake intimacy.** Generated text reaches for "friend" and "loves" and
  breathless gratitude. Real captions are more specific and more restrained.

The test: read the caption aloud. If it could have come from any photographer's
account, it isn't done. One concrete, true detail — the client laughed at their
own joke, the light did something specific, the boardroom had terrible fluorescents
you worked around — beats a paragraph of polished nothing.

## Say true things

Never invent a client quote, a statistic, a result, or a feeling. Never
manufacture urgency — "only 2 spots left!", "booking out fast!" — unless it is
literally true, because a client who books and finds it wasn't remembers. Social
content is marketing, and the same rule holds as everywhere else in this
toolkit: the photographer's credibility is the asset, and a caption that
overreaches spends it. Use `[CONFIRM: ...]` for anything you're asked to state
that isn't known to be true.

## Consent before you feature anyone

A post built around a specific client's face or story needs that client's okay,
especially for corporate work where the images are licensed to the company, not
released for the photographer's marketing. Flag it: before drafting a post that
centres an identifiable person, confirm the photographer has permission to use
them in their own promotion. This is the same usage question the delivery side of
the business already tracks — don't assume a paid shoot means a marketing release.

## Voice travels, format doesn't

The voice is constant across platforms; the *shape* is not. The same photographer
is recognisably themselves in a tight LinkedIn post and a loose Instagram caption,
but the sentence length, the polish, and the emoji rate flex to fit the room. See
[platforms.md](platforms.md) for how far to bend each one.
